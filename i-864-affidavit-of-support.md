# Form I-864, Affidavit of Support: What It Is and What a Sponsor Must Show

**General information only.** This article explains Form I-864 in plain English for a marriage-based green card case where the spouse is applying from inside the United States. It is not legal advice, and it does not tell any person what to do in their own case. Immigration cases depend on the facts of each person's situation. A person who is unsure about their case should talk to an immigration attorney or a DOJ-accredited representative before filing anything.

## The short answer

Form I-864 is the Affidavit of Support Under Section 213A of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). Most family-based immigrants and some employment-based immigrants use it to show that they have adequate means of financial support and that they are not likely to rely on the U.S. government for financial support. In a marriage-based green card case, the sponsor files Form I-864 as part of the application. The form is a legally binding contract with the U.S. government, so the sponsor's promise is serious. The sponsor must sign the form, and USCIS asks the sponsor to provide evidence of income. In some situations, the sponsor may also provide evidence of assets, or the income of a household member may be used. The current edition of the form is 08/24/26, and beginning October 1, 2026, USCIS only accepts that edition. This article explains which edition to use, what the sponsor agrees to, and what evidence USCIS asks for.

## What Form I-864 is

Form I-864 is the Affidavit of Support Under Section 213A of the INA. The word "affidavit" means a written statement that a person signs and swears is true. In this case, the statement is about financial support. The sponsor uses the form to show that the immigrant has adequate means of financial support and is not likely to rely on the U.S. government for financial support.

Most family-based immigrants use Form I-864. Some employment-based immigrants use it as well. In a marriage-based green card case where the spouse is applying from inside the United States, Form I-864 is part of the application package. The sponsor completes the form, signs it, and provides the evidence that USCIS asks for. The form itself is a promise. It is not only a piece of paper. As explained below, it is a legally binding contract with the U.S. government.

## Which edition of the form to use

USCIS updates its forms from time to time, and it is important to use the edition that USCIS currently accepts. The current edition of Form I-864 is 08/24/26. USCIS published this edition on August 31, 2026.

USCIS gave a 30-day grace period for the older 10/17/24 edition. Beginning October 1, 2026, USCIS only accepts the 08/24/26 edition. It will not process a 10/17/24 edition that is postmarked or submitted on or after October 1, 2026.

There is one point that can be confusing. USCIS does not reject Form I-485 if it is filed with a previous edition of Form I-864. However, if an edition other than 08/24/26 is submitted on or after October 1, 2026, USCIS follows 8 CFR 103.2(b)(8) on failure to submit required initial evidence. In plain terms, this means the application may be treated as missing required initial evidence. Because the rules about editions and dates matter, a person who is not sure which edition to use should check the USCIS Form I-864 page or talk to an immigration attorney or a DOJ-accredited representative.

## The privacy release and credit freezes

The 08/24/26 edition of Form I-864 includes a privacy release. This release authorizes USCIS to request information from one or more consumer reporting agencies. A consumer reporting agency is a company that keeps credit information. USCIS uses this information as part of its review.

This matters if the sponsor has a credit freeze or a security freeze. A credit freeze or security freeze is a tool that blocks access to a person's credit information. If a sponsor has a freeze in place, USCIS may not be able to access the information it needs to assess sufficiency. In that case, the sponsor should respond promptly to requests to release the freeze. A delay in releasing the freeze can slow down the review of the form.

## What the sponsor is agreeing to

Form I-864 is a legally binding contract with the U.S. government. A contract is an agreement that can be enforced by law. When the sponsor signs Form I-864, the sponsor is making a legal promise to the U.S. government, not only to the immigrant.

The promise relates to means-tested public benefits. Means-tested public benefits are government benefits that are given based on a person's income or financial need. If the sponsored person receives means-tested public benefits, the agency that granted the benefits can request repayment from the sponsor. If the sponsor fails to repay, the agency may sue the sponsor. If the agency sues, the sponsor is liable for the cost of the benefits, legal fees, and other associated costs.

This is one reason the form should not be signed without care. The sponsor is taking on a real financial responsibility. A person who is considering signing as a sponsor, as a joint sponsor, or as a substitute sponsor should understand this responsibility before signing. If the responsibility is not clear, the person should talk to an immigration attorney or a DOJ-accredited representative.

## Evidence the sponsor provides

USCIS asks the sponsor to provide evidence with Form I-864. The evidence is meant to show the sponsor's income and financial situation. The following paragraphs describe the evidence that USCIS lists on its Form I-864 page.

### Tax returns and income documents

The sponsor provides a copy of their individual federal income tax return, including W-2s, for the most recent tax year. A W-2 is the form an employer gives an employee each year showing wages paid and taxes withheld. If the sponsor was not required to file a tax return, the sponsor provides a statement or other evidence explaining why they were not required to file.

The sponsor also provides every Form 1099, every schedule, and any other evidence of reported income. A Form 1099 is a form that reports income other than wages, such as income from interest, dividends, or work done as an independent contractor. A schedule is a part of a tax return that reports a specific type of income or deduction.

### Additional evidence a sponsor may submit

A sponsor may also submit the most recent three tax years. A sponsor may also submit pay stubs from the most recent six months. Pay stubs are the documents an employer gives with each paycheck, showing how much the employee was paid. A sponsor may also submit a letter from their employer. These are additional pieces of evidence that can help show the sponsor's income.

### Self-employed sponsors

A sponsor who is self-employed provides a Schedule C, Schedule D, Schedule E, or Schedule F. These are tax schedules that report income from a business or from other sources such as rental property or farming. The schedule a self-employed sponsor uses depends on the type of income. USCIS lists Schedules C, D, E, and F for self-employed sponsors.

### Active-duty military sponsors

A sponsor who is on active duty in the U.S. armed forces or the Coast Guard, and who is sponsoring a spouse or child, may use 100% of the Federal Poverty Guidelines. The Federal Poverty Guidelines are the income levels published by the U.S. government that are used to measure poverty. The sponsor in this situation provides proof of active military status. This is a specific rule for active-duty sponsors of a spouse or child. Other sponsors should check the USCIS Form I-864 page or talk to an immigration attorney or a DOJ-accredited representative about the income level that applies to them.

### Household member income

In some cases, the income of a household member is used to help meet the support requirement. When a household member's income is used, a separate Form I-864A is filed for each person whose income is used. Form I-864A is the Contract Between Sponsor and Household Member. Each household member whose income is counted must have their own Form I-864A.

### Assets

A sponsor may also provide evidence of assets. Assets are things of value that a person owns, such as savings, property, or investments. When assets are used, the sponsor provides documentation of the location of each asset, who owns it, the date it was acquired, its value, and any liens or liabilities on it. A lien is a legal claim on property, and a liability is a debt owed. USCIS asks for this detail so that it can understand the true value of the assets.

### Joint sponsors and substitute sponsors

In some cases, a joint sponsor or a substitute sponsor is involved. A joint sponsor is a person who agrees to support the immigrant together with the main sponsor. A substitute sponsor is a person who takes the place of the main sponsor in certain situations. A joint sponsor or substitute sponsor provides proof of their status as a U.S. citizen, a lawful permanent resident, or a U.S. national. A lawful permanent resident is a person who has been given the right to live permanently in the United States. A U.S. national is a person who owes permanent allegiance to the United States but is not a U.S. citizen.

## Signing the form

The form must be signed. USCIS rejects any form that is not signed. This is a simple point, but it is important. A sponsor should check that the form is signed before it is submitted. An unsigned form will not be accepted, and that can cause delay.

## Filing fee

USCIS charges a filing fee for Form I-864. This article does not state the fee amount, because fees can change. To find the current fee, see the USCIS Fee Schedule page. A person should check the fee on the USCIS website before filing, and should not rely on a fee amount found in an older article or on another website.

## When to talk to an attorney or a DOJ-accredited representative

Form I-864 involves a legal contract and financial evidence. There are situations where a person should talk to an immigration attorney or a DOJ-accredited representative before filing. These include:

- If the sponsor is not sure which edition of the form to use, or whether the form they have is the right one.
- If the sponsor has a credit freeze or a security freeze and is not sure how to respond to a USCIS request to release it.
- If the sponsor is considering signing as a joint sponsor or a substitute sponsor and does not understand the responsibility.
- If the sponsor's income comes from more than one source, or if the sponsor is self-employed and is not sure which schedules to provide.
- If a household member's income will be used and the sponsor is not sure how Form I-864A works.
- If the sponsor has questions about what happens if the sponsored person receives means-tested public benefits.

An immigration attorney or a DOJ-accredited representative can give advice about a person's own case. This article cannot do that. It only explains what the form is and what USCIS asks for.
